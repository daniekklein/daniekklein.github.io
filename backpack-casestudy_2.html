The Problem

Most backpacks are designed
for one thing.

People who move between work, travel, and everyday life are forced to own multiple bags — switching between them depending on context. This results in inefficiency, unnecessary consumption, and constant frustration.

The question became: what if a single backpack could intelligently adapt to all three contexts without compromise?

Research

Understanding
the landscape

A visual inspiration web was created using existing professional backpacks to understand common styles, layouts, and design choices. This showed that most professional bags focus on minimal aesthetics but often lack flexibility and quick access.

User behaviour research at school revealed a key insight: despite different preferences, a common issue was that frequently used items ended up hard to reach. This highlighted the need for a backpack that offers both openness and structure.

Process

From sketch

Sketching → Paper models → Full-scale mock-up → CLO3D → Sewing

Step 01 — Develop

Paper models

To understand shape and volume, paper models were made to translate flat sketches into three-dimensional forms.

Step 02 — Digital

CLO3D modelling

All individual parts were designed in CLO3D, defining shapes using measurements and digital pattern construction.

Step 03 — Make

Sewing & testing

The digital design was translated into a physical prototype, testing material choices and construction methods in reality.

Conclusion

A backpack is quite a difficult product to design and make — which makes it quite the challenge.

The final design proves that combining a roll-top system with a front suitcase-style opening improves both access and flexibility. This solves common problems such as digging through the bag and not being able to adjust volume.

As my first project working extensively with textiles, material and construction experimentation played a significant role. The current design should be seen as a prototype that proves the concept, not as a final product.
